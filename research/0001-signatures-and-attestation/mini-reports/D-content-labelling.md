# Area D: Content Labelling — Signed Assertions About Content

## 1. PICS: the architecture worth recovering

PICS 1.1 was published by W3C as two Recommendations on 31 October 1996: **Rating Services and Rating Systems (and Their Machine Readable Descriptions)** (Miller, Resnick, Singer) and **Label Distribution: Label Syntax and Communication Protocols** (Miller ed., Krauskopf, Resnick, Treese). A third document, **PICS Signed Labels (DSig) 1.0**, reached Recommendation on 27 May 1998, adding signature extensions to PICS 1.1 labels with two named signature suites (DSA/SHA-1, RSA/MD5).

The architecturally interesting move is the split the first document makes. A **rating service** is "an individual, group, organization, or company that provides content labels for information on the Internet." A **rating system** "specifies the dimensions used for labeling, the scale of allowable values on each dimension, and a description of the criteria used in assigning values." The rating system is published *separately, as its own machine-readable document* under the MIME type `application/pics-service`, declaring for each category a short wire name (`transmit-as`), a human-readable `name` and `description`, and a value domain (`min`/`max`, or enumerated `label`/`value` pairs).

A label then *cites* its rating system by URL — the service identifier — rather than carrying the vocabulary inline:

```
(PICS-1.1 "http://www.gcf.org/v2.5" labels on "1994.11.05T08:15-0500"
 until "1995.12.31T23:59-0000" for "http://w3.org/PICS/Overview.html"
 ratings (suds 0.5 density 0 color/hue 1))
```

The consequence is late binding of meaning. A user agent meeting `suds 0.5` from a vocabulary it has never heard of can dereference `http://www.gcf.org/v2.5`, retrieve the machine-readable description, and render a correct human-readable interface — category name, scale, criteria — without a software update and without W3C having blessed that vocabulary. The vocabulary is a *resource on the web*, not a clause in a spec.

The second separation is **label bureaus**: "a computer system which supplies, via a computer network, ratings of documents. It may or may not provide the documents themselves." The distribution spec defines an HTTP query protocol — `u=` for the target URL(s), `s=` for the desired rating service(s), `opt=` for the query type — returning `application/pics-labels`. Third parties could assert things about content they neither authored nor hosted, and clients could shop among assessors.

## 2. POWDER: the RDF-native successor

W3C's Protocol for Web Description Resources reached Recommendation on **1 September 2009** as three documents — *Description Resources* (Archer, Smith, Perego), *Grouping of Resources*, and *Formal Semantics* — plus a Primer and Test Suite as Working Group Notes. POWDER kept PICS's central premise: descriptions "are always attributed to a named individual, organization or entity that may or may not be the creator of the described resources." It added an IRI-set algebra so one description could cover a whole site, and a defined transformation of description resources into RDF/OWL.

Note the timing. On **24 November 2009** — eleven weeks after POWDER became a Recommendation — W3C retired the PICS specifications and republished them carrying the note: *"This document is currently not maintained. PICS has been superseded by the Protocol for Web Description Resources (POWDER)."*

## 3. Why both failed — and they failed differently

**PICS did not fail technically; it was consumed by a political fight.** The Communications Decency Act (1996) was struck down in *Reno v. ACLU* (1997), and PICS was immediately repositioned as the self-regulatory alternative to statute — promoted at a July 1997 White House summit. The civil-liberties response was the ACLU white paper **"Fahrenheit 451.2: Is Cyberspace Burning?"** (7 August 1997), which argued that ratings-and-blocking infrastructure would banish controversial speech to the margins more effectively than a statute could. W3C and Resnick counter-argued in the *PICS, Censorship, and Intellectual Freedom FAQ* and the W3C Note *PICS Statement* (1 June 1998). The debate consumed the standard's reputation.

Meanwhile the supply side never materialised. The GVU 10th WWW User Survey (April 1998) found roughly **82% of webmasters had rated none of their pages**, and **70.5% planned to rate none**. Both dominant vocabularies, RSACi and SafeSurf, depended on *self*-labelling — voluntary work with no payoff to the labeller and real liability exposure if the label was wrong. The institutional carrier limped on: RSAC's vocabulary passed to the **Internet Content Rating Association** (founded 12 May 1999 in London, backed by Microsoft, IBM, AOL Europe and the Bertelsmann Foundation), which was absorbed into the Family Online Safety Institute in 2007, and whose **rating system was discontinued in October 2010**.

**POWDER failed quietly.** It attracted no censorship controversy and no users. W3C's TAG proposed obsoleting PICS, CC/PP and POWDER together (`w3c/transitions#86`, opened 31 August 2018, from an April 2018 TAG decision in Tokyo): POWDER's labelling function "did not take off as expected," and W3C "does not believe PICS has sufficient market relevance to continue recommending that the community implement it" — "good efforts, but didn't meet their goals." At last check the issue is *still open, awaiting an AC vote* — eight years on, which is its own adoption evidence.

The shared root cause: both were description infrastructures with no forcing function on either side. Nobody had to label, and almost no user agent consumed labels (IE3's Content Advisor was effectively the only mainstream consumer). PICS additionally had a *demand-side* problem POWDER inherited: labels only matter if something acts on them.

## 4. C2PA / Content Credentials

The current technical specification is **Content Credentials 2.4, April 2026** (Wikipedia gives 21 April 2026; the spec itself says only "April 2026" and the 2.4 index page carries no date). Version 2.3 was announced 23 February 2026; 2.2 was 1 May 2025. The 2.4 suite comprises Content Credentials, Content Credentials JSON (crJSON, new), Attestations, and a Soft Binding API, plus guidance documents.

Structure: a **manifest** is "one or more assertions (including content bindings), a single claim, and a claim signature." An **assertion** is "a data structure which represents a statement either made by the signer or gathered at claim generation-time, concerning the asset." A **claim** is the "digitally signed and tamper-evident data structure that references a set of assertions." Embedding is via **JUMBF (ISO 19566-5:2023)** superboxes, with format-specific guidance for BMFF, PDF, ZIP-based formats, and — new in 2.4 — HTML and structured text.

**External manifests** (§11.4) and **soft bindings** (§9.3) allow a manifest to live apart from the asset and be recovered by perceptual match "even if the underlying bits differ"; §2.4.1 defines a **Durable Content Credential** as one discoverable via soft binding in a manifest repository. 2.4 adds a receipt assertion recording repository storage.

The **UX Recommendations** are a separate document, and it is **frozen at 2.2 while the technical spec has advanced to 2.4** — the 2.4 index still links the 2.2 UX guidance. It defines three validation states — **well-formed** (bindings intact, assertions conform), **valid** (well-formed plus valid signature), **trusted** (valid plus signer on a supported Trust List) — with L1–L4 progressive disclosure from a pin-shaped icon to forensic detail, and with display recipes for degraded states ("The identity of the signer can't be verified"; "these Content Credentials contain incomplete provenance"; hide entirely if not well-formed). It explicitly warns about the implied-truth effect of partial labelling and about ambiguous phrasing like "Made with AI."

**Adoption.** Leica M11-P (26 October 2023) was the first production camera; Sony (α1, α7S III, March 2024 firmware) and Canon (EOS R1, R5 Mark II, 17 July 2025) followed; Google Pixel 10 signs by default. Nikon's Z6III implementation (August 2025) was withdrawn after a vulnerability allowed unauthentic content to carry a valid signature, and its certificates were revoked. The **C2PA Conformance Program** and official Trust List soft-launched 4 June 2025; the Interim Trust List froze on 1 January 2026. As of mid-2026 **no dedicated camera has achieved Conformance Program compliance** — several shipped against v1.4. C2PA claims "more than 6,000 members and affiliates" with live applications (self-reported). Platform and newsroom claims circulate widely but I could not source them primarily — see §7.

**Government guidance.** NSA, with ASD's ACSC, the Canadian Centre for Cyber Security and NCSC-UK, issued the Cybersecurity Information Sheet *Content Credentials: Strengthening Multimedia Integrity in the Generative AI Era* on 29 January 2025, focused on preserving unaltered provenance metadata across the media lifecycle.

## 5. Human factors: the demand side still looks like PICS

Three findings matter.

**Provenance can reduce trust in truthful content.** The CSCW study *Examining the Impact of Provenance-Enabled Media on Trust and Accuracy Perceptions* (N=595, US/UK) found provenance lowered trust in deceptive media — especially composites — but also *overcorrected*, shifting perceptions away from truth for some non-deceptive media. Incomplete or invalid validation states significantly moved judgements, sometimes causing participants to disbelieve honest media. The authors conclude users "confuse media credibility with the orthogonal (albeit related) concept of provenance credibility."

**"Downward shift of trust" and "human washing" are participant coinages, not authors' constructs** — a precision point worth preserving. Both appear in Moruzzi et al., *Content Authenticities* (ACM C&C '25, Best Paper; 34 expert participants, online workshop), and both are attributed to participant (30). Participants worried that "overemphasizing AI-mediated content might lead to a 'downward shift of trust' in media more broadly, and this could result in audiences dismissing legitimate media as fake." The converse: "the absence of a label might lead to the assumption that no AI was involved," giving rise to what participant 30 called **"human washing"** — the paper glosses this against the better-known "AI washing," noting the term is "usually reserved to cases in which AI is deceptively programmed and designed to look and sound like humans" (citing Wired, 2024). The paper's own framing is that *intent* data may be the mitigation, and that "authenticity" is plural ("post-authenticity"), not a single boolean.

**Indicators are unevenly perceivable.** Ide, Park, Mink & Sharma, *Signals of Provenance* (arXiv 2505.16057, May 2025; N=28: 15 sighted, 13 blind/low-vision) document: menu-aided AI labels inaccessible to screen readers due to poor heading structure and inconsistent placement; low engagement with platform labels in favour of unreliable content-based cues (title, description); cognitive overload from competing audio streams; and visual-only designs (watermarks, overlay text, rotating labels) offering nothing to blind participants. The paper does **not** use the phrases "downward shift" or "human washing."

## 6. Regulatory drivers — the forcing function PICS lacked

**EU AI Act Article 50** applies from **2 August 2026**: providers of AI systems generating synthetic audio, image, video or text must mark outputs "in a machine-readable format and detectable as artificially generated or manipulated," and deployers must disclose deepfakes. Penalties reach €15m or 3% of worldwide turnover. The European Commission published the **Code of Practice on marking and labelling AI-generated content on 10 June 2026** — voluntary, signatory-based — which requires digitally-signed, time-stamped metadata or imperceptible watermarking, prohibits intentionally stripping existing markings, requires public detection systems, and mandates standardised EU visual icons. It points at **C2PA** as the digitally-signed-metadata solution and references **CAWG metadata assertions**. A detection-interoperability deadline falls on 2 February 2027.

**California SB 942** (AI Transparency Act), as amended by **AB 853** (signed 13 October 2025), became operative **2 August 2026** — deliberately realigned from 1 January 2026 to match the EU timeline. It requires covered GenAI providers (>1m monthly users) to offer free AI-detection tools, visible manifest disclosures and embedded **latent** disclosures; AB 853 extends obligations to large online platforms, code/product distributors, and **capture-device manufacturers**.

This is the decisive structural difference from PICS. PICS asked webmasters to label voluntarily and 82% declined. C2PA's labellers are model vendors and device makers who now face statutory marking duties.

## 7. Analytical core: does C2PA reproduce the PICS separation?

**No. C2PA bakes its vocabulary into the specification.**

The evidence is direct. §6.2.2 of the 2.4 spec states that "the list of publicly known labels can be found in Chapter 18, *C2PA Standard Assertions*." Chapter 18 enumerates the vocabulary in the spec document itself: `c2pa.actions`, `c2pa.ingredient`, `c2pa.metadata`, `c2pa.hash.data`, `c2pa.hash.boxes`, `c2pa.soft-binding`, `c2pa.thumbnail`, `c2pa.watermarked`, `c2pa.cloud-data`, `c2pa.asset-ref`, `c2pa.depthmap`, and (new in 2.4) `c2pa.ai-disclosure` and `c2pa.environmental-sustainability`. Adding a category means shipping a new version of the specification — and the record shows exactly that: 2.4 was needed to add an AI-disclosure assertion, at the moment regulation demanded one.

C2PA does have an extensibility mechanism, but it is **namespacing without self-description**. §6.2.1: "Entity-specific namespaces shall begin with the Internet domain name for the entity similar to how Java packages are defined (e.g., `com.litware`, `net.fineartschool`)," with a versioning convention (`com.example.label.v2`). That gives collision-free names. It does **not** give a dereferenceable, machine-readable declaration of what the categories are, what values they permit, or how to phrase them for a human — the four things a PICS `application/pics-service` document supplied. The spec defines no human-readable descriptions for assertion labels at all; presentation is delegated to implementers via the separate (and version-lagging) UX Recommendations. A validator meeting `com.litware.something` can verify the signature over it and then has nothing to show the user.

Compare the failure mode. In PICS, an unknown vocabulary degrades to "fetch the description and render it." In C2PA, an unknown assertion degrades to "signed opaque blob." That is a real regression in extensibility, hidden behind the fact that C2PA's cryptography is far better than DSig 1.0's.

**Partial recovery exists, outside the spec.** The **Creator Assertions Working Group** — a working group of the Decentralized Identity Foundation since March 2025 — publishes assertion definitions at cawg.io independently of C2PA: identity, metadata, training-and-data-mining, endorsement. This is institutionally the PICS pattern (a separate body publishing a vocabulary that labels cite), and the EU Code of Practice now cites CAWG assertions alongside C2PA. But CAWG specs are **prose documents requiring hard-coded implementer support**, not machine-readable rating-system descriptions a client can dereference at validation time. Notably, the 2.4 technical specification makes **no mention** of CAWG or of any external assertion registry.

**The label-bureau half is also unrecovered.** C2PA's external manifests, soft bindings and manifest repositories superficially resemble label bureaus — content and assertion stored apart, reunited by lookup. But the assertions in a recovered manifest still come from the production chain that signed the asset. There is no C2PA equivalent of the PICS `u=`/`s=` query — "give me every assertion any party has made about this asset, and let me choose whose assessment I trust." Third-party assessment (a fact-checker, a rights body, a newsroom disputing an image) has no first-class place in the manifest model. C2PA's trust model is a **Trust List of signers**, which answers "is this signer accredited?" — a question about keys — not PICS's "which assessor's vocabulary do I want applied?" — a question about content. Given that area D is defined as assertions about content rather than about keys, C2PA has quietly drifted back toward the key question.

**The recommendation this survey should carry:** the recoverable idea is not PICS's syntax, its politics, or DSig's crypto. It is the **self-describing, separately published, dereferenceable vocabulary** — a machine-readable document declaring categories, permitted values, and human-readable names and descriptions, cited by URL from the label that uses it. C2PA has the cryptography PICS lacked and, thanks to Article 50 and SB 942, the deployment mandate PICS never had. What it has given up is the one thing PICS got right. And the human-factors literature suggests the cost is concrete: "downward shift of trust" and "human washing" are both failures of *interpretation*, and interpretation is exactly what a published vocabulary with human-readable descriptions is for.
